  FDR Portal (SIH26265): Problem Report and
  Winning Strategy
  Problem statement: Streamline of Security Deposit / FDR Portal (AICTE, Smart
  Automation theme)


  1. The problem in plain words
  AICTE approves technical institutions. For some approvals, an institution must lock up
  money with a bank as a guarantee, either as a security deposit or a Fixed Deposit Receipt
  (FDR). That money is held until the institution has met its obligations, after which it is
  released or refunded.

  AICTE's Regulation Bureau therefore has to track, for every institution, one or more
  financial instruments: who issued them, how much, when they were issued, when they
  mature, whether they are genuine, whether they need renewal, and whether a release
  request is valid.

  Today this is done through scattered records, manual correspondence and multiple
  tracking mechanisms. The result is that nobody has a reliable, current view of which
  deposits are healthy, which are about to lapse, which are unverified, and which are
  waiting on an action.

  The ask is a single secure portal that manages the whole lifecycle of each deposit,
  reminds people before things go wrong, and keeps a trustworthy record of everything.


  2. The lifecycle the portal must cover
   1. Submission. The institution submits the instrument details and supporting documents
      (FDR copy, deposit confirmation and so on).
   2. Scrutiny and verification. An AICTE official reviews the record, flags missing or
       inconsistent items, and verifies the instrument through bank-issued verification where
       that is supported.
   3. Monitoring. The system tracks validity and maturity dates and sends configurable
      reminders before deadlines. Overdue or unresolved cases are flagged.
   4. Renewal or replacement. The institution submits a renewed or replacement
      instrument, and the system keeps a complete history of changes.

  4. Hidden signals in the wording
  These phrases tell you what the problem-setters care about beyond the list:

         "Distinguish between information submitted by an institution and information
         independently confirmed by an authorized bank." This is a deliberate trust
         requirement. It is the single most important idea in the statement.
         "Wherever supported" / "where technically feasible and formally authorized." They
         expect integrations to be designed for, not necessarily live. Mocking is acceptable if
         you are honest about it.
         "Configurable reminders." Rules must be adjustable, not hard-coded.
         "Subject to applicable regulations... authorization by the competent authority."
         Release must be gated by approvals. Software must support officials, not replace
         them.
         "Demonstrate measurable improvements." They want numbers: record accuracy,
         verification turnaround, timely renewal monitoring, manual effort reduction, visibility
         of pending cases.
         "Testing should cover scenarios such as..." They name the test cases: new FDR
         submission, validity tracking, approaching maturity, renewal, discrepancy
         identification, authorized release. Treat this as the demo script.


  5. What most teams will do, and why that is not enough
  The typical submission will be an upload form, a table of FDRs, email reminders before
  expiry and a dashboard with a few charts. It will tick boxes 1, 2, 4 and 8 and be hard to tell
  apart from the others.

  To stand out, you need an organising idea, depth on the parts others skim (verification,
  escalation, audit, release) and a demo that proves it works.

  6. Organising idea: every FDR has a trust level and a clock
  Build the product around two questions an official actually asks:

   1. How much of this record is proven, and how much is just claimed?
   2. What is about to go wrong, and who has to act on it?

  Everything in the product ladders up to one of these.


  7. Signature features

  7.1 Trust tiers on every field
  Every data point carries a badge: Institution-claimed, Document-attached or Bank-
  confirmed. When tiers disagree (the claimed amount differs from the bank's response),
  the system raises a discrepancy card showing both values side by side. This directly
  implements the distinction the statement asks for and makes it visible.


  7.2 Mock bank verification service
  Build a small separate service that accepts FDR number, bank and amount and returns
  confirmed, mismatch, source unavailable or pending. Store the source, timestamp and
  result for every call. State clearly that this is a stand-in for authorized bank integrations,
  designed for plug-in.


  7.3 Fast-forward clock
  A simulated "today" control for the demo. Jump ahead 45 days and show reminders firing,
  an FDR flipping to expiring, an unresolved case escalating up the officer chain and a
  lapsed instrument being flagged. A time-based problem is best demonstrated by time
  passing.


  7.4 Instrument passport
  One page per FDR with a full timeline of events, attached documents, verification history
  and the renewal chain (old FDR to replacement FDR). This satisfies the complete-history
  requirement in the most visible way.

  7.5 Risk-ranked "my day" queue
  Instead of only counts, give each official a ranked worklist ordered by amount at risk, days
  to maturity and missing verification. This is what a busy official would use daily, and it
  shows product thinking beyond the checklist.


  7.6 Release workflow with a conditions checklist
  Release or refund requests carry the conditions that must be met, a multi-level approval
  chain, and an automatic block on release while a discrepancy or pending verification is
  open. This reflects the statement's insistence on competent-authority authorization.


  7.7 Tamper-evident audit log
  Chain each log entry with a hash of the previous one and show a "log integrity: verified"
  indicator. It is inexpensive to build and memorable on screen. It supports requirement 9
  convincingly.


  7.8 Institution self-service view
  A separate view for institutions showing what they have on file, what is expiring and a
  one-click path to upload a renewal. This shows the portal serves both sides, not only
  officers.


  7.9 Configurable rules screen
  Let an admin change reminder offsets (for example 90, 60, 30 and 7 days), the escalation
  ladder and required documents per instrument type. This answers "configurable
  reminders" and shows the system is not hard-coded.


  7.10 Realistic domain detail
  Include details an insider would recognise: lien marking, bank confirmation letters,
  discrepancy reasons such as amount mismatch, wrong beneficiary name or date
  inconsistency, and a release approval chain with named roles. This signals you understand
  how the process works in practice.


  8. Roles
       Institution user: submits instruments, uploads renewals, requests release, views own
       status.

       Scrutiny officer: reviews records, runs verification, records observations, flags
       discrepancies.
       Senior approver (competent authority): approves or rejects release and handles
       escalations.
       Admin: manages users, rules and reminder settings.
       Auditor (read-only): views the audit trail and reports.


  9. Data model (core entities)
       Institution (id, name, region, contact)
       Instrument (id, institution, type, reference number, issuing bank, amount, issue date,
       maturity date, status, trust tier per field)
       Document (id, instrument, type, file, uploaded by, timestamp)
       Verification record (id, instrument, source, request, response, result, timestamp)
       Renewal link (old instrument, new instrument)
       Release request (id, instrument, conditions, approvals, status)
       Reminder rule and escalation rule (offset days, recipient role, channel)
       Event log (id, actor, action, before and after values, timestamp, previous hash, hash)
       User and role


  10. Architecture outline
       Frontend: React with role-based views and dashboard.
       Backend API: Node or Python, with role-based access control and an events table.
       Database: PostgreSQL.
       Scheduler: a job that evaluates rules against the simulated or real date and creates
       alerts and escalations.
       Mock bank service: a separate small API.
       Storage: object storage for documents with encryption at rest.
       Notifications: in-app plus email (a sandbox or console output is fine for the demo).
       Security: authentication, role checks on every endpoint, document access logging,
       encryption in transit and at rest.

  11. Build order
   1. Data model, authentication and roles.
   2. Instrument submission and document upload.
   3. Officer review queue and observations.
   4. Mock bank service and verification flow with trust tiers.
   5. Rules engine, scheduler and simulated clock.
   6. Alerts, escalation and the "my day" queue.
   7. Renewal chain and instrument passport.
   8. Release workflow with conditions and approvals.
   9. Dashboard and reports.
 10. Audit log hashing and integrity indicator.
 11. Seed data and polish.

  Cut scope from the end of this list before you cut polish from the start.


  12. Seed data
  Create about 60 FDRs across a spread of states: healthy, expiring soon, matured,
  renewed, mismatched with the bank, pending verification, release requested and
  released. Include a few deliberately tricky cases, such as an amount mismatch, a renewal
  that arrives late and a release request blocked by an open discrepancy. Use synthetic
  data only.


  13. Demo script (about 6 minutes)
  Follow the statement's own test scenarios in order:

   1. New FDR submission. An institution submits an instrument and documents.
   2. Verification and discrepancy. The officer runs bank verification, a mismatch is caught
       and the discrepancy card shows claimed versus confirmed.
   3. Validity tracking. Show the dashboard with active, expiring and matured counts.
   4. Approaching maturity. Fast-forward the clock, reminders fire, the "my day" queue
       reorders.
   5. Escalation. An unresolved case escalates up the chain.

   6. Renewal. The institution uploads a replacement, the renewal chain links old and new,
      and the passport shows the history.
   7. Authorized release. A release request passes its conditions checklist and approval
      chain. Show it blocked once while a discrepancy is open, then unblocked.
   8. Audit. Show the audit trail and the integrity indicator.

  Close with a slide that maps all ten expected-solution items to features.


  14. Proving measurable improvement
  The statement asks for measurable gains. Run a simple before-and-after comparison on
  your seeded data, clearly labelled as simulated:

       Steps and time per case under a manual spreadsheet-and-email process versus the
       portal.
       Days to detect a lapsing FDR.
       Share of lapsing FDRs caught before expiry.
       Time from submission to verification outcome.
       Records with missing or inconsistent fields detected automatically.

  Present these as illustrative figures from a stated simulation, not real-world claims.


  15. Evaluation criteria checklist
  Before submitting, confirm each of these is visible in the demo or deck:

       All ten expected capabilities mapped to features
       All six named test scenarios demonstrated
       Institution-submitted versus bank-confirmed distinction visible
       Configurable reminders and escalation
       Complete history and audit trail
       Role-based access shown with at least two roles
       Measurable improvement slide
       Integration approach described (mock now, authorized bank and AICTE systems later)

  17. Pitch angle
  Open with the problem in one sentence: AICTE holds crores in deposits, and today nobody
  can say at a glance which are proven, which are about to lapse and who has to act. Then
  show the two ideas, trust tiers and the clock, and let the demo carry the rest. Close with
  the mapping slide and the improvement numbers.
